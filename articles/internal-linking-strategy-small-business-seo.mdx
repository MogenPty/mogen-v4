A business website can contain useful [service pages](/articles/tag/service-pages), detailed articles and answers to common customer questions while still being difficult to navigate.

  One reason is that its pages do not connect to one another in a clear, useful way.

  A visitor may read an article but have no obvious route to the related service. A valuable service page may be several clicks away from the homepage. An older article may remain published even though no other page links to it.

  [Internal linking](/articles/tag/internal-linking) helps solve these problems by connecting related pages within the same website.

  Good internal links help people discover relevant information and help search engines find and understand relationships between pages. They also create a more logical route from a visitor's initial question to the information or service that meets their needs.

  For small businesses, this is a practical SEO improvement because it can often begin with pages that already exist.

  This guide explains how to review your website's internal links, build a useful structure and avoid changes that make navigation or content less helpful.

  ## What are internal links?

  An internal link connects one page on your website to another page on that same website.

  For example, an article explaining [website maintenance](/articles/tag/website-maintenance) might link to a related service page. That service page could link to a frequently asked questions page, while the FAQ could direct visitors to an enquiry form.

  These links serve two related purposes:

  - They help visitors discover information relevant to their current question.
  - They help crawlers discover pages and understand how those pages relate to one another.

  Google explains in its [link best practices documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/links-crawlable) that links help Google find pages and assess relevance. It also recommends meaningful anchor text so that people and search engines can better understand the destination.

  Internal linking is not a trick for forcing rankings. It is part of making a website coherent, navigable and discoverable.

  ## 1. Start with the pages that matter most to the business

  Before adding links, identify the website's important pages.

  For a [small business](/articles/tag/small-business), these may include:

  - The homepage.
  - Core service pages.
  - Important product or offering pages.
  - Contact and enquiry pages.
  - Essential policy or customer-information pages.
  - High-value educational articles.
  - Genuine location pages, where the business has useful and accurate location-specific information.

  Not every page has equal importance. A primary service page may be central to the business's commercial goals, while an old announcement may be useful only for historical reference.

  Create a simple page inventory with each URL, its purpose, its audience and the action a visitor should be able to take.

  You can then ask whether each important page is easy to discover from a relevant part of the website.

  ## 2. Organise related content into useful groups

  A clear [website structure](/articles/tag/website-structure) often groups content around services, products or important customer questions.

  Imagine a business that offers website development.

  Its main service page might explain the offering and provide a route to an enquiry. Supporting articles could answer questions about choosing a website architecture, preparing for a website launch and planning ongoing maintenance.

  Those supporting articles can link to the service page when doing so helps the reader. The service page can also link to relevant guides for people who need more information before making a decision.

  This creates a useful relationship between the main offering and the information that supports it.

  A simplified structure might look like this:

  ```text
  Homepage
  ├── Web Development
  │   ├── Choosing a Website Architecture
  │   ├── Website Launch Checklist
  │   └── Website Maintenance Guide
  ├── SEO
  │   ├── Search Performance Guide
  │   └── Internal Linking Guide
  └── Contact
  ```

  This is an example, not a required site structure. The appropriate arrangement depends on the services, content and customer journeys of the actual business.

  Avoid creating unnecessary pages just to fill out a diagram. Each page should have a clear purpose and provide information that is useful to its intended audience.

  ## 3. Link from relevant articles to relevant services

  Educational content often attracts visitors who are researching a problem rather than looking for a provider immediately.

  Internal links can help those visitors find the next useful step.

  For example, an article about planning a business website could link to a [web development](/services/web-development) service page. A guide to improving search visibility might link to an SEO service page when the connection is genuinely useful.

  The link should make sense in the context of the sentence.

  Avoid inserting the same commercial call to action into every paragraph. A link should help readers continue their journey, not interrupt the information they came to understand.

  Ask three questions before adding a link:

  1. Would a reader reasonably want to visit the destination at this point?
  2. Does the destination provide the information or action implied by the link?
  3. Can the link be expressed clearly without forcing an awkward keyword phrase?

  If the answer to these questions is yes, the link is more likely to serve both readers and the website's structure.

  ## 4. Use descriptive anchor text

  Anchor text is the clickable text of a link.

  Compare these examples:

  - Vague: "Click here."
  - More descriptive: "Read our website maintenance guide."
  - Vague: "Learn more."
  - More descriptive: "Explore the web development service."

  Descriptive anchor text sets expectations. Visitors can better understand where a link leads before selecting it.

  Google recommends meaningful anchor text in its [link best practices guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/links-crawlable).

  This does not mean that every link should contain an exact-match target keyword. Repeating the same phrase unnaturally across the website can make content less readable.

  Use language that accurately describes the destination. Natural variations are appropriate when they make sense in context.

  For example, an article could link to a service page using "website development options" in one sentence and "our web development service" elsewhere. The destination should still match the reader's expectation.

  ## 5. Find important pages with few or no internal links

  A page that has no internal links pointing to it is often called an orphan page.

  Such a page may still be accessible through an external link, a sitemap or another route, but it can be harder for visitors and crawlers to discover through the normal website structure.

  Review your important URLs and identify pages that are not linked from relevant navigation, service pages or supporting content.

  Start with pages that matter to the business:

  - Important service pages.
  - Recently published articles.
  - High-value guides.
  - Pages that have useful search impressions but are difficult to reach.
  - Pages that support a key customer journey.

  Add links only where a genuine relationship exists.

  An article should not link to a page merely to avoid being orphaned if that link would confuse readers. Reconsider whether the article belongs in the current site structure, whether it needs a better parent page or whether its content should be consolidated.

  Google recommends ensuring that important pages can be reached through crawlable links from other discoverable pages. Its [developer's guide to Search](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/get-started-developers) also discusses the role of links and sitemaps in helping Google find URLs.

  ## 6. Make sure links are technically usable

  A link can look correct on screen while being difficult for a crawler or assistive technology to interpret.

  For ordinary website navigation, use semantic links with a valid destination rather than relying solely on click handlers attached to generic elements.

  Review the following:

  - Links point to the intended URL.
  - Important links work on mobile and desktop.
  - Link text describes the destination.
  - Links are not hidden behind broken interactive elements.
  - Important pages do not rely solely on a sitemap for discovery.
  - Redirects and broken links are handled appropriately.
  - Navigation remains usable with a keyboard.

  Google explains that its crawlers generally parse links implemented as HTML anchor elements with an `href` attribute. See its [crawlable link guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/links-crawlable).

  If your website uses a JavaScript framework, verify the actual rendered output and live behaviour rather than assuming that a visually clickable element is a crawlable link.

  ## 7. Use navigation and breadcrumbs where they help

  Internal links do not all need to be embedded in article paragraphs.

  A website's main navigation helps visitors find its major sections. Footer links can provide access to useful secondary pages. Breadcrumbs can show the relationship between a page and its parent categories.

  Each component has a different purpose.

  Main navigation should prioritise the destinations that matter most to users. It should not become a directory of every URL on the website.

  Breadcrumbs are particularly useful on larger sites with meaningful category or hierarchy structures. A small site with a handful of pages may not need a complicated breadcrumb system.

  The objective is consistency and clarity. Visitors should be able to understand where they are, how the current page relates to the rest of the site and where they can go next.

  ## 8. Be careful with location-specific pages

  Local businesses sometimes create separate pages for every town, suburb or nearby community in the hope of attracting more search traffic.

  That approach is not automatically useful.

  A location page should provide a genuine reason for existing. It may explain a distinct service arrangement, useful local information or a real difference in how the business serves that audience.

  Avoid producing multiple pages that simply swap one place name for another while repeating the same content.

  For example, a business that serves customers in [Maboloka](/locations/maboloka), [Soshanguve](/locations/soshanguve) and [Centurion](/locations/centurion) should describe its actual service arrangements accurately. It should not imply a physical office, local team or specialised service in an area unless that claim is true.

  Where location pages are appropriate, connect them to relevant service pages and useful supporting information. Where a single service page adequately describes the offering, adding several nearly identical location pages may create unnecessary duplication.

  Google's guidance on [helpful, reliable, people-first content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) encourages publishers to create content that benefits their intended audience rather than producing pages primarily to attract search traffic.

  ## 9. Audit internal links in five practical steps

  A small business can begin with a spreadsheet and a focused review.

  ### Step 1: List the important URLs

  Record the homepage, core services, major resources and other pages that support the business's main goals.

  ### Step 2: Record each page's purpose

  Note the audience, the question it answers and the next useful action.

  ### Step 3: Check incoming links

  Identify which pages link to each important destination. If a page is difficult to find, determine where a relevant link could be added.

  ### Step 4: Review link quality

  Confirm that the anchor text is descriptive, the destination is correct and the link appears in a useful context.

  ### Step 5: Test and record changes

  Check the live website after implementation. Record the URLs changed and the reason for each change so that future reviews have a reliable starting point.

  For larger websites, a crawler can help identify broken links, orphan pages and internal linking patterns. For smaller sites, a manual review of the most important pages may be sufficient to identify useful improvements.

  ## 10. Measure outcomes without expecting a guaranteed ranking change

  Internal linking can improve discoverability and navigation, but no particular link arrangement guarantees higher rankings.

  After making changes, review whether important pages are easier to reach and whether the links provide a better experience for visitors.

  Depending on your measurement setup, useful checks include:

  - Whether important pages can be crawled and indexed as intended.
  - Whether internal links resolve correctly.
  - Whether visitors use the links to reach related content.
  - Whether important pages receive relevant search impressions and clicks over time.
  - Whether the website makes it easier for visitors to reach an enquiry or other meaningful action.

  Google Search Console can help investigate indexing and search performance. Analytics tools can help examine visitor behaviour after they arrive, provided the relevant events are configured correctly.

  Avoid attributing every change in search performance to internal linking alone. Content updates, demand, competition, technical changes and other factors may also influence results.

  ## Common internal linking mistakes

  ### Linking to unrelated pages

  A link should provide useful context or a logical next step. Irrelevant links can confuse readers.

  ### Repeating exact-match keywords everywhere

  Descriptive anchor text is useful, but forced repetition can make a website feel unnatural. Write for the reader first.

  ### Adding too many navigation items

  More links do not automatically produce better navigation. Keep important destinations prominent and organise secondary content sensibly.

  ### Ignoring old content

  Older articles may still provide value, but their links can become outdated when services, URLs or site structure change. Review them periodically.

  ### Assuming a sitemap replaces internal links

  A sitemap can help search engines discover URLs, but it does not replace a coherent navigation structure. Important pages should also be reachable through relevant internal links.

  ## Frequently asked questions

  ### How many internal links should a page have?

  There is no universal ideal number. Use enough links to support navigation and provide useful context without overwhelming the page. Google's guidance recommends avoiding excessive linking when it makes the experience less useful.

  ### Do internal links directly guarantee better rankings?

  No. They help people and search engines discover and understand pages, but rankings depend on many factors. The value of internal linking is in building a coherent, useful website rather than guaranteeing a particular result.

  ### Should every article link to a service page?

  Only when the service is relevant to the article and the link helps the reader. Educational content should remain useful in its own right.

  ### Should I remove every orphan page?

  Not automatically. First establish whether the page is still needed, whether it has a legitimate purpose and whether it should be linked from another relevant page. Some pages may be intentionally excluded from normal navigation.

  ## Conclusion

  Internal linking is a practical way to make a website easier to navigate and its important pages easier to discover.

  Start by identifying the pages that matter most, connect related content naturally and use descriptive anchor text. Review orphan pages, check that important links are technically usable and make sure the structure reflects the way customers actually look for information.

  For a small business, a thoughtful internal linking strategy can make better use of existing content while creating a clearer path from an initial question to a useful answer or service.

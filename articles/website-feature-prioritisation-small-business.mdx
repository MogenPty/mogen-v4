  When planning a new website, it is easy to become distracted by everything modern [websites](/articles/tag/websites) can do.

  Online bookings, automated quotations, customer accounts, product catalogues, live chat, payment gateways, interactive calculators and sophisticated animations can all sound attractive. The difficulty is deciding which features will genuinely help your business and which will add cost and complexity without solving an important problem.

  For a [small business](/articles/tag/small-business), the right website is not necessarily the one with the most functionality. It is the one that helps customers understand the offering, make informed decisions and take the next appropriate step.

  Whether you operate in [Maboloka](/locations/maboloka), [Soshanguve](/locations/soshanguve), [Centurion](/locations/centurion) or another South African community, the same principle applies: start with the business requirement rather than a list of fashionable technologies.

  This guide explains how to prioritise website features, estimate their operational implications and create a realistic development roadmap.

  ## 1. Begin with the business problem

  Before requesting a feature, describe the problem it is supposed to solve.

  Consider these examples:

  - Customers repeatedly telephone to ask about prices.
  - Staff spend too much time arranging appointments manually.
  - Visitors cannot find the services offered by the business.
  - Enquiries arrive without enough information to prepare quotations.
  - Customers want to understand delivery arrangements before ordering.
  - The business cannot easily update important information on its website.

  Each problem suggests a possible improvement, but not necessarily a particular technical solution.

  If customers cannot find prices, a clear pricing page might be sufficient. A complex interactive quotation calculator may be unnecessary.

  If appointments are difficult to coordinate, a booking system might help. However, a simpler enquiry form may be more appropriate if the business needs to assess each request before confirming a booking.

  Define the problem first. Then compare the available ways to address it.

  ## 2. Identify the people who will use the feature

  A feature should be evaluated from the perspective of both the customer and the business.

  A visitor might need to compare services, request a quotation or determine whether the business operates in their area. Staff might need to review enquiries, update information or coordinate appointments.

  Write down the main users and their tasks.

  For each proposed feature, ask:

  - Who needs it?
  - What are they trying to accomplish?
  - How frequently will they use it?
  - What happens if the feature is unavailable?
  - Is there a simpler way to complete the task?
  - What support will users need?

  These questions can reveal that a feature solves an occasional inconvenience rather than an important business problem.

  They can also uncover requirements that were initially overlooked. For example, a booking system may appear straightforward until the business realises that appointments require manual approval, deposits or additional preparation.

  The objective is to understand the complete task, not just the visible interface.

  ## 3. Separate essential features from optional enhancements

  One useful approach is to divide proposed features into three groups.

  | Priority | Meaning | Examples |
  |---|---|---|
  | Essential | Needed for the website's primary purpose | Clear service information, working contact details and an appropriate enquiry route |
  | Valuable enhancement | Improves an established customer or business process | Online booking, structured quotation requests or a searchable catalogue |
  | Later or unnecessary | Adds complexity without a sufficiently clear current benefit | A custom customer portal for a business that has no recurring customer-account workflow |

  This classification is not permanent. A booking feature may be optional for one business and essential for another.

  A retailer processing regular online orders has different requirements from a consultant who qualifies enquiries before agreeing to a meeting.

  Make the priority decision using the actual business model rather than copying another company's website.

  ## 4. Evaluate the value of each feature

  A feature can be attractive without producing a meaningful business benefit.

  Estimate its potential value by considering the problem it addresses, the number of people affected and the amount of effort it could save.

  A simple assessment might use these questions:

  **Customer value:** Will the feature make it easier for customers to understand, evaluate or purchase the offering?

  **Operational value:** Will it reduce repetitive work, prevent avoidable mistakes or improve coordination?

  **Commercial value:** Could it support more qualified enquiries, completed transactions or customer retention?

  **Frequency:** How often does the problem occur?

  **Evidence:** Have customers requested the feature, or is the idea based mainly on assumption?

  A business does not need sophisticated financial modelling for every website decision. Even a short written comparison can prevent money being spent on low-priority functionality.

  Where practical, estimate the cost of the current problem. If staff spend several hours each week handling a repetitive task, automation may deserve attention. If a task happens twice a year, a manual process may remain more economical.

  ## 5. Consider the total cost, not just development

  A website feature can create ongoing responsibilities after its initial implementation.

  For example, an online booking system may require:

  - Calendar configuration.
  - Availability management.
  - Appointment notifications.
  - Cancellation handling.
  - Customer support.
  - Integration maintenance.
  - Security and access reviews.

  An online shop can introduce product management, payment reconciliation, delivery coordination, stock updates, returns and customer enquiries.

  Before approving a feature, consider its full lifecycle.

  | Cost area | Questions to ask |
  |---|---|
  | Initial development | What must be designed, configured and tested? |
  | Subscription costs | Does the feature depend on a paid third-party service? |
  | Maintenance | Who handles updates, errors and compatibility issues? |
  | Staff time | Who manages the information and daily workflow? |
  | Security | What data is collected, and who can access it? |
  | Training | Will employees need instructions or ongoing support? |
  | Failure handling | What happens when the feature or provider is unavailable? |

  A low initial price does not necessarily mean a low total cost.

  Similarly, a more expensive feature may be worthwhile if it reliably removes a substantial operational burden. The decision should be based on the complete cost and expected benefit.

  ## 6. Do not automate a process that has not been defined

  Automation can make a good process more efficient, but it can also make a poorly defined process harder to manage.

  Imagine a business that wants automated quotation generation.

  Before developing the feature, it should understand how quotations are prepared, which information is required, how prices are calculated and who approves exceptions.

  If staff currently follow different procedures, the business should establish a consistent process first.

  Otherwise, the website may automate inconsistent decisions or produce quotations that need extensive correction.

  The same principle applies to booking, customer onboarding, payment handling and internal notifications.

  Define the process, establish the rules and then determine which steps should be automated.

  ## 7. Make accessibility part of the original scope

  [Accessibility](/articles/tag/accessibility) should not be treated as a cosmetic improvement that can always be added later.

  The World Wide Web Consortium's Web Accessibility Initiative explains that accessible websites help people with disabilities perceive, understand, navigate and interact with web content. Accessibility can also benefit people using mobile devices, experiencing temporary limitations or working with different connection conditions.

  Its [Introduction to Web Accessibility](https://www.w3.org/WAI/fundamentals/accessibility-intro/) recommends incorporating accessibility considerations into the development process.

  When assessing a proposed feature, consider whether it can be used with a keyboard, whether its instructions are understandable, whether important information is available to assistive technologies and whether errors can be identified and corrected.

  An elaborate interface that excludes some customers may be less useful than a simpler, well-implemented alternative.

  Accessibility is relevant to the whole website, including navigation, forms, interactive controls, documents and dynamically displayed information.

  ## 8. Account for mobile devices and real operating conditions

  A feature should work in the environment in which customers are likely to use it.

  South African customers may access a business website using different devices, network conditions and levels of digital confidence. A complex interface can be frustrating if it loads slowly, consumes unnecessary data or requires precise interactions on a small screen.

  Test the complete customer journey rather than only inspecting the desktop design.

  Ask whether a visitor can:

  - Find the relevant information quickly.
  - Understand what the feature does.
  - Complete the task on a phone.
  - Recover from a mistake.
  - See confirmation that the action succeeded.
  - Continue if an optional integration is unavailable.

  Keep the interface proportionate to the task.

  A simple service enquiry should not require a visitor to create an account, complete a lengthy profile and navigate several screens unless those steps are genuinely necessary.

  ## 9. Consider privacy and security implications

  Features that collect or process information introduce additional responsibilities.

  A basic information page may need little personal information. An account system, booking platform or online shop may process customer details, transaction records or other information that requires more careful handling.

  Before adding such functionality, determine:

  - What information is collected.
  - Why it is required.
  - Where it will be stored.
  - Which people or providers can access it.
  - How it will be protected.
  - How long it needs to be retained.
  - What happens when a customer requests an appropriate correction or deletion.

  Avoid collecting information simply because a feature makes it technically possible.

  If a third-party provider processes data, review its role, relevant terms and security arrangements. Ensure that the implementation reflects applicable South African requirements, including those relevant to personal information.

  Privacy and security are part of feature planning, not merely final testing.

  ## 10. Build in stages

  A phased approach allows a business to establish a useful foundation before investing in more complex functionality.

  **Stage one: Core website**

  Publish the essential business information, explain the offering, provide clear navigation and establish a reliable way for customers to make contact.

  **Stage two: Improve the customer journey**

  Review actual enquiries and customer questions. Improve information, clarify service options and address recurring friction.

  **Stage three: Add justified functionality**

  Introduce booking, payments, customer portals or automation when the business has a defined need and can support the resulting workflow.

  **Stage four: Review and refine**

  Examine usage, errors, customer feedback and operating costs. Keep useful features, improve underperforming ones and retire functionality that no longer serves a purpose.

  Staging does not mean that every project should be launched with minimal functionality. It means that each stage should have a clear purpose and that future expansion should be based on genuine requirements.

  ## 11. Prepare a feature brief before development begins

  A short feature brief can make discussions with a developer more productive.

  For every important feature, record:

  - **Problem:** What business or customer problem needs to be solved?
  - **User:** Who will use the feature?
  - **Expected outcome:** What should become easier or more reliable?
  - **Requirements:** What must the feature do?
  - **Dependencies:** Does it require an external service, data source or integration?
  - **Responsibilities:** Who will maintain and operate it?
  - **Acceptance criteria:** How will the business decide that it works correctly?
  - **Priority:** Is it essential for launch or suitable for a later phase?

  This brief does not need to be a lengthy technical specification. Its purpose is to establish a shared understanding before implementation begins.

  ## Frequently asked questions

  ### Should every business website have online booking?

  No. Booking is useful when customers need to select an appointment and the business can maintain accurate availability. A quotation or enquiry process may be more appropriate when each request requires individual assessment.

  ### Is it better to add every feature during the initial build?

  Not automatically. Features that are essential to the primary business purpose should be planned from the beginning. Other features can be introduced later when their value and operating requirements are better understood.

  ### How do I know whether a website feature is worth the cost?

  Compare its expected benefit with development, subscription, maintenance, support and staff costs. Where possible, identify a measurable business outcome and review whether the feature achieves it.

  ### Can a simple website support a growing business?

  Yes. A well-planned website can start with essential functionality and expand as the business's requirements change. Simplicity is useful when it supports the task rather than restricting customers or staff.

  ## Conclusion

  Website development decisions should begin with business needs, customer tasks and operational realities.

  Prioritise features that address meaningful problems, consider the full cost of maintaining them and build accessibility, privacy and mobile usability into the original plan.

  A staged approach gives a business room to grow without committing prematurely to functionality it may not need.

  The objective is not to build the largest possible website. It is to build a useful digital tool that supports the business today and can evolve when there is a clear reason to do so.

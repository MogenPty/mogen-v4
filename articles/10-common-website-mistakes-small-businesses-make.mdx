  A business website can look attractive and still make it difficult for customers to understand the offer, trust the business or get in touch.

  For a [small business](/articles/tag/small-business) serving [Maboloka](/locations/maboloka), [Soshanguve](/locations/soshanguve), [Centurion](/locations/centurion) or nearby communities, those problems can mean missed opportunities. A prospective customer might arrive on a phone, look for a service, check whether the business covers their area and decide within seconds whether to continue.

  Good website development focuses on making that journey clear and dependable. Here are ten common mistakes, why they matter and how to address them.

  ## 1. Making visitors guess what the business does

  The homepage is often the first place visitors look for an explanation of the business. If the headline is vague, people may not understand whether the service is relevant to them.

  Phrases such as "innovative solutions for a changing world" sound polished but reveal very little about the actual offer.

  **How to fix it:** Explain what the business does, who it helps and what problem it solves. Use plain language and support the headline with a concise description of the main services.

  For example, a business offering electrical repairs should make that service immediately understandable rather than relying on a generic statement about quality and excellence.

  ## 2. Designing for desktop and treating mobile as an afterthought

  A website may look excellent on a large monitor but become frustrating on a phone. Navigation can be cramped, text difficult to read and buttons too close together.

  Mobile usability matters to businesses across Maboloka, Soshanguve, Centurion and surrounding areas because customers may research services while away from a computer.

  **How to fix it:** Design responsive layouts from the beginning. Test navigation, forms, text sizes, buttons and contact links on real mobile devices.

  Check the entire experience, not only whether the page technically fits the screen.

  The South African Government Communication and Information System's [website guidance](https://www.gcis.gov.za/guidelines/website/cms) recommends mobile-first design and performance suitable for users on limited-bandwidth connections.

  ## 3. Using oversized images and unnecessary effects

  Large photographs, autoplay videos, excessive animations and unnecessary third-party scripts can increase loading time and make a website harder to use.

  A business does not need to remove every visual element. It needs to use them purposefully.

  **How to fix it:**
  - Resize images to the dimensions actually required.
  - Compress photographs without making them visibly poor quality.
  - Use suitable modern image formats.
  - Avoid autoplaying large videos unless they serve a clear purpose.
  - Remove scripts and plugins that provide little value.
  - Load below-the-fold media when it is needed.

  Measure performance before and after making changes. Guessing which asset is responsible for a slow page can waste development time.

  ## 4. Making navigation unnecessarily complicated

  Visitors should not have to search through several menus to find services, prices, contact details or important policies.

  Too many menu items can overwhelm people, while vague labels can make navigation unpredictable.

  **How to fix it:** Group related information into a small number of understandable categories. Give important pages descriptive names and ensure the navigation works on both desktop and mobile.

  If the business offers several services, organise them logically instead of placing every detail in the main menu.

  The [GCIS website guidance](https://www.gcis.gov.za/guidelines/website) provides further recommendations on navigation and structuring information logically.

  ## 5. Trying to explain every service on one crowded page

  A one-page website can be a sensible starting point for a business with a simple offer. Problems arise when too many unrelated services, conditions and explanations are forced into the same space.

  Visitors may struggle to find the information relevant to their particular need.

  **How to fix it:** Give important services their own pages when they require substantial explanation. Each page should describe the service, who it suits, what customers can expect and how to enquire.

  A business offering several distinct professional services may benefit from individual pages. A small business with one main service may need only a focused page with clear sections.

  Build according to the complexity of the offer rather than choosing a page count simply to make the website look substantial.

  ## 6. Hiding the next step

  A visitor may understand the service and still leave because it is unclear what to do next.

  A page can contain plenty of information without making the next action obvious.

  **How to fix it:** Give each important page a clear next step, such as requesting a quotation, calling the business, asking a question or booking an appointment.

  Use descriptive button labels. "Request a quotation" is more informative than a button that simply says "Click here".

  Place calls to action where they make sense in the content. Repeating the same promotional message after every paragraph is not necessary.

  ## 7. Publishing forms and contact links without testing them

  A contact form that appears to work but fails to deliver messages can be worse than having no form at all. Similarly, an incorrect telephone number or broken WhatsApp link can prevent a customer from making contact.

  **How to fix it:** Test every enquiry path after development and after significant changes.

  Check that:
  - Forms validate required fields correctly.
  - Successful submissions produce a clear confirmation.
  - Notifications reach the intended recipient.
  - Error messages help users correct problems.
  - Telephone and messaging links open the intended destination.
  - Spam controls do not block legitimate enquiries unnecessarily.

  Send test enquiries from a mobile device and a desktop computer. Verify receipt rather than assuming that a success message means the entire process works.

  ## 8. Publishing inaccurate business information

  Outdated opening hours, old telephone numbers, incorrect prices and unclear service coverage can undermine confidence.

  This is particularly important when a business mentions several places. A business should not imply that it operates from an office in Centurion if it does not have an eligible business location there.

  **How to fix it:** Review contact details, service descriptions, opening hours, pricing and location information regularly.

  If the business serves Maboloka, Soshanguve, Centurion and surrounding communities, describe the areas it genuinely covers and explain any relevant travel, delivery or appointment arrangements.

  Do not publish testimonials, credentials or project examples that cannot be substantiated.

  ## 9. Ignoring accessibility

  Small design decisions can make a website unnecessarily difficult for visitors with disabilities or those using different devices and assistive technologies.

  Common problems include poor colour contrast, missing labels on forms, links that do not describe their destinations and navigation that cannot be used with a keyboard.

  **How to fix it:** Use a logical heading structure, readable text, meaningful link labels, appropriate image alternatives and clearly labelled form fields. Check keyboard navigation and ensure that important information does not depend on colour alone.

  The [GCIS accessibility guidance](https://www.gcis.gov.za/guidelines/website/website-accessibility) discusses [accessibility](/articles/tag/accessibility) principles and WCAG guidance. Businesses should assess the standards and legal obligations applicable to their own websites rather than assuming that one checklist guarantees compliance.

  ## 10. Launching without a maintenance plan

  A website is not a finished product that can safely be ignored forever. Content becomes outdated, dependencies need updates and business requirements change.

  Neglect can also leave backups untested, forms broken and security issues unresolved.

  **How to fix it:** Establish a maintenance routine that includes:
  - Software and dependency updates, where applicable.
  - Regular backups and restoration checks.
  - Form and contact-link testing.
  - Security monitoring appropriate to the technology.
  - Content and contact-detail reviews.
  - Performance checks.
  - Review of important customer journeys.

  The frequency depends on the website. A static information site has different maintenance needs from an online store or a site that processes customer accounts.

  ## A practical website review for local businesses

  A business does not have to rebuild its website every time it identifies a problem. Start with the issues that create the most friction.

  | Priority | What to inspect | Why it matters |
  |---|---|---|
  | High | Service clarity and contact details | Visitors need to understand the offer and reach the business. |
  | High | Mobile layout and enquiry forms | Customers must be able to use the website on their devices. |
  | High | Broken links and failed submissions | Technical failures can block enquiries. |
  | Medium | Image sizes and unnecessary scripts | Performance improvements can make pages more responsive. |
  | Medium | Navigation and service-page structure | Visitors should find relevant information quickly. |
  | Ongoing | Accessibility, backups and updates | Reliability requires continued attention. |

  For a business serving Maboloka, Soshanguve, Centurion or nearby areas, begin by testing the pages customers are most likely to visit before making contact.

  ## Frequently asked questions

  ### Does every small business need a multi-page website?

  No. A focused one-page website can work for a simple offer. Multiple pages become useful when services, customer needs or supporting information require separate explanations.

  ### Should I redesign my entire website if it is slow?

  Not necessarily. Measure the problem first. Image optimisation, script reduction, caching or a specific code improvement may resolve the main issue without requiring a complete redesign.

  ### How often should I review my website?

  Check important contact paths and business information regularly. The schedule for technical updates, backups and performance reviews should reflect the website's technology, complexity and risk.

  ### Do website mistakes affect customer trust?

  They can. Confusing service information, broken contact options and inaccurate details make it harder for customers to decide whether a business is suitable and dependable.

  ## Conclusion

  A successful small business website does not need every available feature. It needs to explain the offer, work reliably on different devices and make the next step straightforward.

  By addressing these ten mistakes systematically, businesses serving Maboloka, Soshanguve, Centurion and surrounding communities can improve the experience they provide without spending money on changes that do not solve a real problem.

  A [small business](/articles/tag/small-business) planning to improve its website often faces the same question: should it publish more blog articles or create more pages explaining its services?

  Both can be valuable, but they serve different purposes.

  A service page helps prospective customers understand what a business offers, whether it meets their requirements and how to take the next step. A blog article can answer questions, explain a problem, compare options or help someone make a more informed decision.

  Publishing the wrong type of page for a searcher's needs can make a website less useful. Publishing many similar pages without meaningful differences can also create unnecessary maintenance and content-quality problems.

  For businesses in [Maboloka](/locations/maboloka), [Soshanguve](/locations/soshanguve), [Centurion](/locations/centurion) and elsewhere in [South Africa](/articles/tag/south-africa), the objective should be to build a website that answers real customer questions and explains the actual services available.

  This guide explains how to decide what to publish first and how [service pages](/articles/tag/service-pages) and articles can work together.

  ## 1. Understand the different jobs of each page

  Start by distinguishing between a page intended to explain an offering and a page intended to answer a question.

  A service page might explain:

  - What the service includes.
  - Which business problems it addresses.
  - Who it is suitable for.
  - What information the customer should prepare.
  - How the process works.
  - What factors affect cost or scope.
  - How to request further information.

  A blog article might explain:

  - How a process works.
  - What common mistakes to avoid.
  - How two approaches compare.
  - What a technical term means.
  - Which questions to ask before making a decision.
  - How to assess whether a solution is appropriate.

  These purposes can overlap, but they should not be confused.

  If someone searches for a business that provides a particular service, an informative service page may be the most appropriate destination. If someone wants to understand a problem before choosing a provider, an educational article may be more useful.

  Neither page type is inherently superior. The question is which page best serves the visitor's current need.

  ## 2. Build the essential service pages first

  If a business website does not clearly explain what the business does, adding more articles may not address its most important content gap.

  Begin by identifying the main services that the business genuinely provides.

  For each service, consider whether a dedicated page would help customers understand it more clearly than a brief paragraph on the homepage.

  A useful service page should explain the offering in enough detail for a prospective customer to decide whether it is relevant.

  For example, a [web development](/services/web-development) service page could explain the kinds of websites the business builds, the information needed to begin a project, how the development process works and which functionality may require additional planning.

  A professional service page should not merely repeat a list of keywords. It should answer practical questions and set accurate expectations.

  Google's [Search Essentials](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials) recommends creating helpful, reliable, people-first content and using language that people would use when looking for the information or service.

  A page should be written for the intended customer, with search visibility treated as a means of helping relevant people find it.

  ## 3. Match the page to the searcher's intent

  [Search intent](/articles/tag/search-intent) describes what someone is trying to accomplish through a search.

  For [content planning](/articles/tag/content-planning), it is useful to think about several broad patterns.

  | Search behaviour | Likely need | Suitable page type |
  |---|---|---|
  | Looking for a provider | Understand the service and how to enquire | Service page |
  | Learning about a problem | Understand the issue and possible solutions | Educational article |
  | Comparing options | Evaluate differences and trade-offs | Comparison article or detailed service content |
  | Preparing to buy | Check suitability, scope, process or costs | Service page, pricing information or buying guide |
  | Seeking a practical answer | Follow a process or solve a specific problem | Tutorial or how-to article |

  These are planning guidelines, not rigid classifications. A search can have more than one intent, and the best page depends on the actual question.

  Review the language customers use in enquiries, sales conversations and support requests. Search results can also help you understand what kinds of pages are already serving a topic.

  Avoid deciding that a page must be an article simply because the keyword tool labels a query as informational. Consider the full question and the outcome the visitor wants.

  ## 4. Give every important service a clear destination

  A website should make it easy for visitors to discover its main services.

  If a business offers several distinct services, consider whether each deserves a separate page.

  A dedicated page can be useful when the service has its own audience, process, requirements or customer questions.

  However, not every minor variation needs a separate URL.

  Imagine a business that offers website development and ongoing website maintenance. If these are genuinely different services with distinct scopes and customer expectations, separate pages may make sense.

  If the business merely changes the wording of an otherwise identical offering, additional pages may add little value.

  Use a clear hierarchy:

  - The homepage introduces the business and its main offerings.
  - A services overview helps visitors choose a relevant service.
  - Individual service pages explain specific offerings.
  - Supporting articles answer questions that arise during research and decision-making.

  Internal links should help people move naturally between these pages.

  A blog article about choosing website functionality, for example, could link to a relevant web development service page when readers are ready to discuss a project.

  ## 5. Use blog posts to answer questions the service page cannot cover in depth

  A service page should explain the offering comprehensively, but it does not need to answer every conceivable question.

  Articles can address more specialised subjects without making the main service page unnecessarily long.

  Consider a business that develops websites. Its service page might explain the development process, scope and contact options.

  Supporting articles could explore:

  - How to prepare a website project brief.
  - How to decide which website features are necessary.
  - What to check before a website launch.
  - How website maintenance responsibilities should be assigned.
  - What information a business should prepare before requesting a quotation.

  Each article should provide enough value to stand on its own.

  It should also have a clear relationship to the website's purpose. Publishing articles about unrelated subjects simply because they appear popular can make the site's [content strategy](/articles/tag/content-strategy) less coherent.

  Google's [guidance on creating helpful, reliable, people-first content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) encourages publishers to assess whether content serves an intended audience, offers useful information and provides value beyond what is already available elsewhere.

  The key is not to produce articles merely to increase the number of URLs. It is to address meaningful questions that customers actually have.

  ## 6. Avoid creating unnecessary pages for every keyword variation

  A common content-planning mistake is to create a separate page for every slightly different search phrase.

  Consider a business that offers the same service to the same audience. It may be tempted to create multiple pages with near-identical content because different keywords mention different towns, suburbs or variations of the service name.

  If the pages provide essentially the same information and lead visitors to the same destination, the business may be creating more maintenance work without providing additional value.

  Google's [spam policies for Google Web Search](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) describe doorway abuse, including creating substantially similar pages for specific regions or queries that funnel users towards the same destination without offering meaningful value.

  This does not mean that a business can never create separate location pages or service variations.

  A separate page can be justified when it provides genuinely useful, distinct information for the intended audience.

  For example, different service areas might have different delivery arrangements, operating requirements, appointment options or project examples. The page should explain those differences accurately rather than simply replacing one place name with another.

  The test is straightforward: would the page still be useful if the location keyword were removed from its heading?

  If the answer is no, reconsider whether the page deserves to exist.

  ## 7. Decide when a location-specific page is justified

  Location relevance can matter to businesses that serve customers within defined geographical areas.

  However, a location page should reflect the actual service proposition.

  Before creating one, establish:

  - Whether the business genuinely serves the area.
  - Whether customers in that area have distinct requirements.
  - Whether the business can provide useful local information.
  - Whether the page can explain relevant service arrangements accurately.
  - Whether there is enough meaningful content to justify a separate page.

  For a business serving customers in Maboloka, Soshanguve or Centurion, this may involve explaining genuine service availability, relevant appointment arrangements or the types of projects the business can undertake.

  Do not imply a staffed office, physical branch, guaranteed availability or local project history unless those claims are accurate.

  If the service is delivered remotely and the business can work with customers in multiple areas, explain that arrangement honestly. A separate page for every possible town may not be necessary.

  It is better to have a smaller set of accurate, useful pages than a large collection of repetitive location pages.

  ## 8. Plan articles around customer questions

  An effective article plan begins with questions rather than an arbitrary publishing quota.

  Gather ideas from:

  - Questions received during enquiries.
  - Objections raised before customers make decisions.
  - Problems encountered after a purchase.
  - Common misunderstandings about the service.
  - Comparisons customers need to make.
  - Tasks customers must complete before starting a project.

  Group related questions into themes.

  For example, a website development content plan could contain articles about planning, functionality, launch, maintenance and evaluating a developer.

  This approach creates a more coherent collection of resources than publishing unrelated topics without a clear editorial purpose.

  Before commissioning an article, check whether an existing page already answers the same question. If it does, consider improving that page rather than creating another with substantially the same purpose.

  ## 9. Link service pages and articles together

  Service pages and articles should not operate as isolated destinations.

  Internal links help readers discover related information and help search engines understand the relationships between pages.

  Consider a reader who finds an article explaining how to plan a website project.

  The article could link to a web development service page that explains how to discuss project requirements. The service page could link back to the guide as a resource for customers preparing to begin.

  Use descriptive link text that explains what the visitor will find.

  Avoid generic links such as "click here" when a clearer description is possible.

  Internal links should be relevant and useful rather than inserted merely to increase the number of links on a page.

  A sensible structure makes it easier for customers to move from learning about a problem to understanding the available service.

  ## 10. Measure usefulness, not just the number of pages

  Once pages are published, evaluate whether they serve their intended purposes.

  For service pages, useful questions include:

  - Are relevant visitors finding the page?
  - Does it attract enquiries from people seeking the service?
  - Do customers understand the scope before making contact?
  - Are important questions still unanswered?
  - Is the next step clear?

  For articles, consider:

  - Are they attracting relevant readers?
  - Do readers discover related service information?
  - Are the articles being used to answer customer questions?
  - Do they help customers understand the business's offering?
  - Are they accurate and worth maintaining?

  Traffic alone does not establish business value. A page with modest traffic may be important if it helps qualified customers make a decision. A popular article may be less commercially useful if it attracts the wrong audience.

  Avoid rewriting pages solely because their traffic is lower than expected. First determine whether the content, intent, audience and business purpose are aligned.

  ## 11. A practical publishing priority for small businesses

  If resources are limited, use this order as a starting point.

  **First, make the essential information complete.**

  Ensure visitors can understand the business, identify the main services and find an appropriate way to make contact.

  **Second, improve the most important service pages.**

  Prioritise services that are central to the business and ensure each page explains the offering accurately.

  **Third, address significant information gaps.**

  Publish articles that answer important customer questions that the service pages cannot reasonably cover in depth.

  **Fourth, connect the content.**

  Add relevant internal links between articles, service pages and other useful resources.

  **Fifth, review and improve.**

  Monitor how the pages perform, listen to customer feedback and update content when information or requirements change.

  This sequence is a practical starting point rather than a universal rule. A business with strong service pages but an important unanswered customer question may reasonably prioritise the article first.

  The important principle is to address the most meaningful content gap instead of publishing simply to meet a quota.

  ## Frequently asked questions

  ### Are blog posts more important than service pages for SEO?

  Neither is universally more important. Service pages explain what the business offers, while articles often address informational needs. The appropriate balance depends on the business, its customers and the questions it needs to answer.

  ### Should every service have its own page?

  A dedicated page is useful when a service has a distinct purpose, audience, scope or set of requirements. Minor variations that do not provide additional value may be better explained on an existing page.

  ### How many blog posts should a small business publish?

  There is no universal number that guarantees success. Prioritise useful, accurate articles that serve a clear purpose and can be maintained properly.

  ### Should I create separate pages for Maboloka, Soshanguve and Centurion?

  Only when each page serves a genuine audience and provides meaningful, accurate information about the relevant service or area. Do not create near-identical pages merely to target different place names.

  ## Conclusion

  Service pages and blog posts perform different but complementary roles.

  Build service pages that explain the actual offering and help customers take the next step. Use articles to answer relevant questions, explain options and provide useful information before and after a customer begins evaluating the service.

  Keep the website structure clear, avoid repetitive pages and link related content together naturally.

  For South African small businesses, the strongest content strategy is not necessarily the one that produces the most URLs. It is the one that helps the right people understand the business and make better-informed decisions.

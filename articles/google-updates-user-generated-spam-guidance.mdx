A website can attract spam even when its owner publishes useful, original content and follows sensible SEO practices.

Public comment forms, customer review areas, discussion boards, profile pages and user-submitted listings can all become targets for automated submissions and people trying to publish promotional links.

The problem is not limited to the inconvenience of deleting unwanted comments. If a website publishes user-generated pages without appropriate controls, those pages can create low-value URLs, expose visitors to suspicious links and introduce content that the business never intended to publish.

[Google Search](/articles/tag/google-search) Central has recently updated its guidance on preventing user-generated spam.

The documentation update was published on 26 October 2026. Google added guidance about preventing link-shared content from being indexed when it is not intended for Search, and monitoring abuse through [Search Console](/articles/tag/search-console).

This is a documentation update, not an announcement of a new ranking algorithm or a new universal ranking penalty.

The updated guidance is particularly relevant to businesses and platforms that allow visitors or registered users to publish content on their [websites](/articles/tag/websites).

For a [small business](/articles/tag/small-business) serving customers in [Maboloka](/locations/maboloka), [Soshanguve](/locations/soshanguve), [Centurion](/locations/centurion) or elsewhere, the practical question is straightforward: what can visitors publish on the website, and how is that content controlled?

## What counts as user-generated content?

[User-generated content](/articles/tag/user-generated-content), often shortened to UGC, is content created or submitted by people other than the website's publisher.

Examples include:

- Blog comments.
- Public customer reviews.
- Forum discussions.
- User profiles.
- Classified advertisements.
- Community listings.
- Public questions and answers.
- Shared pages created by registered users.
- Content submitted to a publishing platform.

Not every business website has these features.

A basic brochure website with a homepage, service pages and a contact form may have very little publicly published user-generated content.

However, a contact form can still attract automated spam submissions. A business should protect the form with appropriate validation and anti-abuse controls, even when the submissions are not published as public pages.

The distinction matters because the SEO risks are different.

A spam message sitting in a private enquiry inbox is not the same as a spam-filled public page that search engines can discover and index.

Google's [user-generated spam prevention guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/monitor-debug/prevent-abuse) explains that open comment forms and other user-generated content inputs can be abused to publish spammy content on websites.

## What did Google update on 26 October?

Google's Search Central documentation updates page records an update to its user-generated spam prevention guide on 26 October 2026.

The update adds guidance about:

- Blocking indexing of content created for link-sharing rather than public search discovery.
- Monitoring user-generated content abuse through Search Console.

The underlying advice is practical: website owners should distinguish between content that is intended to be discovered in Search and content that exists only for direct sharing, collaboration or utility.

For example, a platform might allow a user to generate a private document or create a page to share with a small group. That page may be useful to the people who have the link, but it does not necessarily need to appear in Google Search.

Google's recommendation is to apply appropriate indexing controls to such content by default.

You can read the announcement in [Google Search Central's latest documentation updates](https://developers.google.com/search/updates).

## 1. Identify where visitors can publish content

Start by reviewing the website's public-facing features.

Do not limit the review to the obvious comment section.

Look for any feature that allows a visitor or registered user to create content that becomes accessible through a public URL.

| Website feature | Potential risk |
| --- | --- |
| Blog comments | Promotional links and automated comments |
| Public reviews | Fake reviews, links and abusive content |
| Member profiles | Spam accounts and low-value profile pages |
| Classified listings | Duplicate or misleading advertisements |
| Discussion boards | Repetitive posts and irrelevant links |
| Shared documents | Pages that should not appear in public search |
| User-created pages | Large numbers of low-value URLs |

Also check whether a third-party service embeds user-generated content into your website.

A business that displays reviews from another platform does not necessarily control the original review system. It should understand what is embedded, what is stored locally and which platform is responsible for moderation.

## 2. Separate public content from private or shared content

Not every page that can be opened through a browser should be indexed by search engines.

Consider three broad categories:

**Public editorial content**

These are pages deliberately created to attract visitors through search, such as service pages, articles and useful public resources.

**Public user-generated content**

These are pages created by users that may be useful to a wider audience, such as approved public reviews, listings or forum discussions.

**Direct-sharing or private utility content**

These pages exist for a specific user or group, such as a shared document, temporary output, collaboration page or private result.

The third category usually does not need to appear in public search results.

Google's updated guide recommends applying appropriate indexing controls to content intended for direct sharing rather than public search discovery.

A business should document which page types belong in each category. This prevents developers from applying the same indexing rule to every page simply because the website has a user-generated content feature.

## 3. Use noindex where pages should not appear in Search

The `noindex` rule tells a compliant search crawler not to include a page in its search index.

It can be implemented through a robots meta element in the page or an `X-Robots-Tag` HTTP response header.

For example:

    ```text
    <meta name="robots" content="noindex" />
    ```

This is useful for pages that need to remain accessible to people with a link but should not appear in Google Search.

However, there is an important technical distinction between indexing controls and crawl controls.

Google needs to crawl a page to discover its `noindex` instruction. If the same URL is blocked in `robots.txt`, Google may not be able to see the page-level instruction.

Therefore, if the objective is to remove a page from Google's index using `noindex`, do not simultaneously prevent Google from crawling that URL.

Google's [guide to blocking search indexing with noindex](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/block-indexing) explains the available methods and how they interact with crawling.

`robots.txt` is useful for controlling crawler access, but it is not a substitute for a page-level `noindex` instruction when Google needs to read that instruction.

Do not apply `noindex` to normal service pages, articles or other content that the business wants search engines to discover.

## 4. Qualify links submitted by users

One reason spammers publish comments and profiles is to place links to other websites.

These links may be promotional, irrelevant or potentially harmful.

Google recommends using the `ugc` value in the `rel` attribute for links that appear in user-generated content.

For example:

    ```text
    <a href="https://example.com" rel="ugc">User-submitted link</a>
    ```

The `ugc` value identifies the link as user-generated content.

Where the website owner does not want to associate the site with an untrusted destination or facilitate crawling through that link, `nofollow` may also be appropriate.

For example:

    ```text
    <a href="https://example.com" rel="ugc nofollow">User-submitted link</a>
    ```

Google explains the available link qualification values in its [outbound link guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/qualify-outbound-links).

Do not add `ugc` to ordinary editorial links simply because they appear in an article. The attribute is intended for links submitted through user-generated content areas.

Also remember that qualifying a link does not remove the spammy content itself. Moderation, account controls and removal of abusive material remain important.

## 5. Moderate suspicious submissions

Automated spam systems often try to publish content immediately after a form or account has been created.

Manual moderation can interrupt this process.

For example, a business could require approval before a new user's first public review or listing appears on the website.

Moderation does not need to be complicated.

A small website might begin with:

- Holding first-time submissions for review.
- Automatically approving trusted contributors after a history of legitimate activity.
- Flagging submissions containing suspicious links.
- Allowing trusted users to report abusive content.
- Removing content that violates the published rules.

Google's guide specifically recommends manual approval for suspicious interactions and explains that moderation can reduce spam on platforms that accept user-generated content.

The business should also publish clear rules explaining what users may submit and what will be removed.

A moderation policy is easier to enforce when users can understand it.

## 6. Look for suspicious account behaviour

A spammer may create many accounts or submit repeated content through different forms.

Google recommends monitoring user interactions and looking for patterns such as:

- Time taken to complete a form.
- Repeated requests from the same IP address range.
- User agents used during registration.
- Similar usernames or submitted values.

These signals can help identify suspicious behaviour.

They should not be treated as automatic proof that a person is a spammer. Legitimate visitors may share networks, use common devices or submit similar information.

A sensible anti-abuse system combines several signals and provides a way to review false positives.

For a small business, useful first steps include:

- Rate-limiting repeated submissions.
- Adding a suitable human-verification challenge where necessary.
- Validating form fields on the server.
- Rejecting malformed or clearly abusive input.
- Keeping a record of suspicious patterns.
- Reviewing repeated abuse before blocking a source.

Google also mentions verification tools such as reCAPTCHA as one option for discouraging automated account creation.

Use verification proportionately. A difficult challenge on every page can create unnecessary friction for genuine customers.

## 7. Monitor Search Console for unusual activity

The updated Google guidance places emphasis on monitoring abuse through Search Console.

Website owners should verify the relevant website or subdomain in Search Console so they can receive notifications about certain manual actions or security issues.

They should also review:

- The Page Indexing report.
- The Search performance report.
- Unexpected increases in indexed URLs.
- Queries that do not match the website's purpose.
- Unusual redirects.
- Suspicious changes to publicly available content.

Imagine a small business website that normally has a few dozen public pages.

If the number of indexed pages suddenly increases dramatically, the owner should investigate rather than assume that the website has become more successful.

New URLs may be legitimate, but they may also indicate that a user-generated feature has been abused.

Google recommends tracking content creation and indexing activity and looking for unusual spikes.

For a website with user accounts or public listings, it may also be useful to track the number of pages created by each account and flag unusual patterns.

Search Console is not a substitute for application-level monitoring. It provides [search visibility](/articles/tag/search-visibility) and indexing information, while the website's own logs and moderation tools help explain what happened.

## 8. Do not remove legitimate customer contributions unnecessarily

Spam prevention should not become an excuse to disable useful interaction.

Genuine reviews, questions and community contributions can help visitors understand a business and make informed decisions.

A business should distinguish between:

- Genuine customer feedback.
- Relevant questions.
- Constructive discussion.
- Promotional spam.
- Automated submissions.
- Abusive or misleading material.

A moderation process should be consistent and transparent.

Do not remove legitimate criticism simply because it is negative. Focus on whether the content violates the published rules, contains abusive material or creates a genuine security or spam concern.

This is particularly important for businesses that rely on public feedback to build trust with customers.

## 9. What should a basic business website do?

A business website without public comments, reviews or user-created pages may not need a complex UGC moderation system.

It should still protect its enquiry forms and monitor for unexpected changes.

A practical checklist includes:

| Area | Recommended action |
| --- | --- |
| Contact forms | Use server-side validation and suitable anti-spam controls |
| Public comments | Moderate submissions and qualify user links |
| Reviews | Use clear publishing and moderation rules |
| Member pages | Review whether public indexing is necessary |
| Shared pages | Keep direct-sharing content out of Search where appropriate |
| Search Console | Monitor indexing and search performance |
| Website logs | Investigate unusual submission and traffic patterns |
| Account creation | Use appropriate verification and rate limits |

If a website has no user-generated public pages, do not introduce unnecessary indexing restrictions simply because Google updated its documentation.

The correct response is to assess the actual features of the website.

A business that has customers in Maboloka, Soshanguve, Centurion or surrounding areas may use a website to collect enquiries, publish reviews or provide customer resources.

Each feature should have a defined purpose.

For example, a service provider might publish genuine customer testimonials but keep private quotation requests and generated documents out of public search results.

Each public page should have a clear reason to exist and should provide value to the intended audience.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Google's October 2026 update a new ranking algorithm?

No. Google's Search Central updates page records a documentation update to its user-generated spam prevention guidance on 26 October 2026. It is not described as a new ranking algorithm update.

### Does every contact form need noindex?

No. A private contact form submission is not automatically a public page. `noindex` is relevant to pages that should remain accessible but should not appear in search results.

### Should all customer reviews be blocked from Google?

No. Genuine public reviews may be useful to visitors. The objective is to prevent spam and control which user-generated pages are appropriate for search discovery.

### Is rel="ugc" enough to prevent comment spam?

No. It qualifies links in user-generated content. It does not replace moderation, spam detection, account controls or removal of abusive submissions.

### Can robots.txt replace noindex?

Not when Google needs to read a page-level `noindex` instruction. If a URL is blocked from crawling, Google may not be able to see that instruction. Use the appropriate control for the intended outcome.

## Conclusion

User-generated content can be valuable, but it needs responsible publishing controls.

Google's October 2026 documentation update is a useful reminder that websites should distinguish between public content intended for search discovery and pages created only for direct sharing or utility.

Businesses that accept comments, reviews, listings or other user submissions should review moderation, link qualification, indexing rules and Search Console monitoring.

Those with simpler websites should focus on protecting their forms and keeping their published content under control.

The objective is not to block every contribution. It is to make sure that what appears on the website is intentional, useful and appropriate for visitors and search engines.
